# Essay Openings — What Works, What Doesn't

Source: College Essay Guy, "How to Start a College Essay" (https://www.collegeessayguy.com/blog/how-to-start-college-essay), logged 2026-07-21. Written for college admissions essays, but the craft transfers to blog posts, newsletter issues, and personal essays. Living log — add new opening types here as they prove themselves.

## Four openings to avoid

1. **The Overly Grand Ambiguous Statement** — "Many great thinkers have existed in our nation's history." Generalized, impersonal, tells the reader nothing about the writer. No connection to the writer = no reason to keep reading.
2. **Going Meta** — "I stare at the blank screen, unsure how to begin…" Writing about the act of writing the piece. Readers see it constantly; it reads as stalling. Can occasionally work, but degree of difficulty is high.
3. **The Quote** — opening with someone else's famous words. The reader wants the writer's thoughts, not a stand-in. (Fine to break deliberately — one of the source's best examples opens with a "secret" framed as a quote.)
4. **The Too-Obvious Thesis** — "I want to be a veterinarian because I care about animals." Spoils the ending in sentence one; the reader can now skip the piece. Know your logline, don't hand it over up front.

## Nine openings that work

1. **The Full Hemingway** — pure image-based description of one moment, no explanation (yet). Dialogue, actions, details speak for themselves. Risky if the reader is never helped to understand why it was described.
2. **The Mini Hemingway** — 1–3 image sentences, then ONE grounding sentence that gives context without giving away where the piece goes. The most reliable opener: creates curiosity, then orients fast. *"…mixing fat lips of fresh cabbages with garlic, salt, and red pepper. That was how kimchi was born every weekend at my home."*
3. **The Twist** — build an expectation about who's talking, then break it. "Growing up, my world was basketball… But that was only me on the surface." Works because it plays with assumptions and shows self-awareness in layers.
4. **The Philosophical Question** — ask something the piece won't (and can't) fully answer: "Does every life matter? Because it seems like certain lives matter more than others." Warning: drifts into Overly Grand territory if not anchored fast.
5. **The Confession** — admit something you could be judged for: "Here is a secret no one in my family knows: I shot my brother when I was six." Vulnerability that raises questions the reader has to stay for.
6. **The Trailer Thesis** — a grounding 1–2 sentences at the END of the first paragraph that previews where the piece goes without spoiling the ending. Signals "you're in good hands."
7. **The Fascinating Concept** — open with an unusual or paradoxical fact/idea that marked a turning point in the writer's thinking ("Crayfish can turn their blood cells into neurons"). Signals curiosity and range; heady rather than vulnerable — don't overdo the geek language.
8. **The Random Personal Fun Fact** — a strange, specific fact about yourself, then why it matters. "Oh, that's cool — where is this going?"
9. **The Shocking Image** — an arresting, hyper-specific image or sentence, then why it matters: "Smeared blood, shredded feathers. Clearly, the bird was dead. But wait…" Sentence rhythm can mirror the content (staccato fragments = anxiety).

## Using this in the skill

- Writer stuck on an opener → propose 2–3 techniques BY NAME from the list, sketched against their material. Don't dump all nine.
- Critiquing a draft opening → check it against the four to avoid first; name the offense if there is one.
- Fit to the writer's voice profile: check which register they favor and propose from that group.
- Newsletter issues can also open with direct reader address ("You know the feeling…") — that's a house pattern, not from this source.
