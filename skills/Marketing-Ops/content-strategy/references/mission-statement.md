## Why this part exists

The mission is one sentence that says who the content is for, what it covers,
and what the reader gets. Every later decision gets checked against it: a topic
that doesn't fit the mission doesn't get written. The framework article cites
CMI research showing that only a minority of B2B programs (39% in the survey
cited) have a documented strategy, and that those who do are roughly twice as
likely to report success. Later CMI surveys put the numbers differently, but
the direction never changes: written down beats "everyone knows".

The mission also does quiet work: once written, it becomes the subscribe CTA,
the blog header, the social bio. You've written your calls to action.

## Method

The formula is **audience + topics + benefit**, in one sentence.

**Audience.** This is broader than the buyer. Content serves two groups:
people who might buy, and everyone the expertise can help. The second group
is where links, shares, and subscribers come from, and without them the first
group never arrives in volume. Name a role or a situation, not a demographic.

**Topics.** The overlap between what the team can teach and what the audience
wants to learn. Common nouns here: tips, advice, insights, strategies,
research, checklists. Research is the strongest. Decide early whether the
program is evergreen or news-driven; they run on different cadences.

**Benefit.** Why they care. What can they do after reading that they couldn't
before? If no benefit is stated, the content has no reason to be read.

A useful variant: "The place where [audience] find [topics] to [benefit]."
Another: "To be the destination for [who], on [what], because [why]."

### Scope test

- Narrow audience, wide topics: fine. Wide audience, narrow topics: fine.
  Wide audience, wide topics: not a mission, a directory.
- Say what you will never write about. If the mission doesn't exclude anything,
  it isn't specific enough.
- Read it aloud as a subscribe pitch. If you wouldn't sign up, rewrite it.

### Where it lives

Top of the blog, every newsletter signup, social bios. Three places, same words.

## Template with worked examples

> Where **[audience]** find **[topics]** to **[benefit]**.

Examples written for this skill (fictional companies):

- Payroll consultancy: *Where finance leads at 50-500 person companies find
  plain answers on payroll, compliance, and headcount cost so they stop losing
  money to mistakes they didn't know they were making.*
- Developer tools vendor: *Where platform engineers find honest write-ups on
  CI, build systems, and toolchain trade-offs to ship faster without babysitting
  pipelines.*
- Regional logistics firm: *Where operations managers in mid-size manufacturers
  find practical guidance on freight, warehousing, and delivery SLAs to keep
  promises to their own customers.*

Each one names a role, a topic set, and an outcome. Each one excludes plenty.

## Questions to ask the user

- Who exactly reads this? Role and situation, not "businesses".
- Beyond buyers, who else could the expertise help, and why would they share it?
- Which topics can the team teach with authority? Which are off-limits?
- After reading a typical piece, what can the reader do that they couldn't before?
- Draft the sentence together. Then: would you subscribe to that?
