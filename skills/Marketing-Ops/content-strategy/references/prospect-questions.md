# Part 4: Write for current prospects

Distilled from Andy Crestodina / Orbit Media. Sources:
https://www.orbitmedia.com/blog/content-strategy-framework/ (part 4),
https://www.orbitmedia.com/blog/what-to-blog-about/ (evil twin, audience
questions, process posts). Own words throughout.

## Why this part exists

The people already in the pipeline are the most valuable audience the content
program has. They have questions, and every unanswered question is friction in
a deal. When you publish the answer, two things happen: sales can send the link
to the prospect who asked, keeping the conversation alive, and every future
prospect with the same question gets the same answer, so the team's messaging
stops drifting. This is the part of the strategy that pays off in weeks, not
quarters.

## Method

### Mine the questions

Sit in on sales calls. Read the inbox threads sales forwards. Watch demo
recordings. Ask each rep: what did prospects ask last month, what did they get
wrong about the offer, what stalled a deal? Also check what visitors type into
the site's own search box and which questions Google shows under "people also
ask" for your commercial phrases. You're looking for repeats and misconceptions,
not one-offs.

### Map question to article

| Question (in the prospect's words) | Who asks | Deal stage | Article title | Sales use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| "Is it cheaper than doing it in-house?" | Finance lead | Evaluation | The real cost of in-house payroll for a 200-person company | Send after first call |

One question, one article. Answer directly in the first paragraph, then show the
reasoning, then link to the service page. These are middle-of-funnel pieces:
specific to your offer, hard for competitors to copy, and natural internal-link
sources for the sales page.

### Process and stance posts

Two related sources of pipeline-relevant topics: explain exactly how you do
something (your process, your checklist, your tools), and take a position on a
question your industry argues about. Both are unique to you, which is what
makes them convincing to a prospect comparing options.

### The evil twin

Take the best-performing how-to and write its mirror: the mistakes version, the
"how to get this wrong" version, the contrarian take. Same knowledge, opposite
angle. It's not a rewrite; it adds new material and a new frame. Two rules: wait
a while before publishing the twin on your own site, or pitch it as a guest post
somewhere else, where it does double duty as part 7.

Examples: "How to choose a payroll provider" becomes "Five ways companies pick
the wrong payroll provider". "Onboarding checklist for new hires" becomes "How
to lose a new hire in their first ten days".

## Checklist

- [ ] Three or more repeated prospect questions captured, in their words
- [ ] Each mapped to a title, a deal stage, and a sales use
- [ ] Each article links to the service page
- [ ] Sales team knows the articles exist and where to find them
- [ ] One evil twin identified for guest-post use

## Questions to ask the user

- What three questions came up in sales conversations last month?
- What do prospects consistently get wrong about the offer?
- Which question, when it comes up, tends to stall or kill a deal?
- Does sales send articles to prospects today? Which ones?
- Which how-to on the site has performed best? (That's the evil twin candidate.)
